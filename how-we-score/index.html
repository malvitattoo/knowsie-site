How Knowsie scores food

Last updated 16 October 2026

No mystery numbers here. A score is only useful if you can see where it comes from, so this page lays out how Knowsie scores food, with the exact points. In the app, each product’s score breakdown shows what took points off, and you can tap any rated ingredient to see why it’s there. The base score is the same for everyone, and brands can’t pay for a better one. Ever.

Every product starts at 100

Points come off for processing, for the ingredients we rate and for added sugar, and chips lose 25 points for being chips. A few kinds of food earn a small bonus. The score never goes below 0 or above 100, and it’s rounded to a whole number at the end.

On top of the points, some ingredients cap the score, and these kinds of food have a ceiling. More on caps and ceilings below.

Processing

Every ingredient in our database has a processing level from 1 to 4:

  • Level 1: food as it comes, like potatoes, oats, milk or eggs.
  • Level 2: lightly processed, like salt, flour, extra virgin olive oil or yogurt.
  • Level 3: processed, like refined sunflower oil or skimmed milk powder.
  • Level 4: ultra-processed, like flavors, maltodextrin or whey protein isolate.

Levels 1 and 2 cost nothing. Each level 3 ingredient costs 0.5 points and each level 4 ingredient 1.5. All of this together can cost up to 35 points.

Frying, extruding and recombining don’t show up in an ingredient list, so we also look at the product as a whole. For that we use its NOVA group, a widely used system that sorts food into four groups by how processed it is. A processed product (NOVA 3) loses 5 points and an ultra-processed one (NOVA 4) loses 14. The NOVA group comes from Open Food Facts. For a product our team has added or corrected, the team sets it, and for one you’ve just photographed, the app works it out from the ingredients. If we don’t know a product’s NOVA group, nothing comes off.

Sugars are left out of the per-ingredient processing points, because they’re counted by amount (see Added sugar). Added vitamins and minerals are left out too.

Rated ingredients

Each ingredient we rate takes off points by its concern level: 2 for Low concern, 5 for Some, 8 for Moderate, and 10 to 15 for High. Together they can take off up to 40 points. Each ingredient counts once per product, however many times the label lists it, and so does a substance that two ingredients share (see What a rating is about). When a label lists an oil blend, like “sunflower and/or palm oil”, we count it once, by its worst oil: here palm oil, which is High concern. The levels are explained below.

Added sugar

Sugar is sugar. Whether the label says sugar, honey, syrup or fruit juice concentrate, we count how much added sugar is in a serving, once per product.

Added sugar in a servingPoints

Over 5 g, up to 10 g−5
Over 10 g, up to 15 g−8
Over 15 g, up to 25 g−12
Over 25 g−15

We use the serving on the label when it’s realistic. Otherwise we use a typical serving for that kind of product, based on the US FDA’s reference serving sizes, or 50 g when we can’t tell what kind of food it is.

Most labels outside the US don’t say how much of the sugar is added, so then we estimate it:

  • From the total sugar on the label. Some of it may come from fruit or milk, so this costs 8 points at most, unless a sugar is the first ingredient.
  • If the label gives no sugar figures at all, from where sugar sits in the ingredient list: 12 points if it’s the first ingredient, 5 if it’s in the first three, and 2 if it’s further down.

The app tells you when a sugar figure is an estimate. Sugar that’s naturally in milk or fruit doesn’t count on its own, and fruit juice concentrate doesn’t count in drinks, jams or fruit spreads. Fermented foods like kombucha, kefir or sauerkraut lose only half the sugar points, because fermentation uses up some of the sugar. Yogurt doesn’t get this discount, because its sugar is usually added after fermentation.

Bonuses

  • Whole food, +5: every ingredient we recognize is level 1 or 2, there’s no rated ingredient and no added sugar, and the product as a whole isn’t processed (NOVA 1 or 2, or unknown).
  • Fermented food, +5: yogurt, kefir, kombucha, sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, kvass and the like, and any product that lists cultures among its own ingredients. It applies even when the product contains additives.

Some ingredients cap the score

However well a product does on everything else, these ingredients set the highest score it can get:

The product containsHighest possible score
1 High concern ingredient49
2 or more High concern ingredients25
3 or 4 Moderate concern ingredients65
5 or 6 Moderate concern ingredients50
7 or more Moderate concern ingredients35

If two caps apply, the lower one counts, and the same goes for a cap and a ceiling. So a product with one High concern ingredient scores 49 at most, in the Limit range, however good the rest of it is.

Some kinds of food have a ceiling

Some foods are a concern for what they are, whatever else is in them. Three kinds have a ceiling: the highest score they can get. A ceiling works like a cap: on its own it doesn’t take points off, it stops the score going higher. Processing, rated ingredients and sugar can still take a product lower, and when a cap is lower than the ceiling, the cap counts.

  • Sugar, honey and syrups: products that are a sugar, such as white or brown sugar, coconut sugar, honey, maple or agave syrup, molasses, or a syrup you dilute into a drink. Sugar is sugar, whatever the shop calls it. Small additions, like vanilla or a flavoring, don’t change that. Food that has sugar in it but isn’t a sugar, like jam, sweets, sauces or ready-to-drink drinks, isn’t in this group: its sugar is counted by amount (see Added sugar). Nor are sugar-free sweeteners, like stevia or erythritol.
  • Chips: potato chips, including ones made from potato dough, like stacked chips or potato rings; tortilla and corn chips; and vegetable chips, all fried or baked with oil. The rest of the chips family counts too: corn and cheese puffs, veggie straws, potato sticks, prawn crackers, popped chips, pita and bread chips, lentil, chickpea and grain chips, fried banana and apple chips, toasted or seasoned coconut chips, and savory rice, corn and multigrain cakes made with oil (5 g of fat or more per 100 g). They lose 25 points, before any cap or ceiling, and then score 75 at most, so no chips reach Low concern. Plainer chips still score higher than heavily flavored ones: plain salted chips (potatoes, sunflower oil and salt, a processed product) score 70, and the flavored chips in our worked example 54. When chips land in Occasional, the line under the score says “A now-and-then snack” instead of “Fine a few times a week”. Popcorn, pretzels, crackers, nuts and plain rice cakes aren’t in the family: they’re scored like other foods. Nor are puffs made without oil, sweet puffs and sweet rice cakes (like chocolate or caramel ones), fries and oven chips, or dried and freeze-dried fruit.
  • Alcoholic drinks: beer, wine, cider, spirits and other drinks with more than 0.5% alcohol by volume score 35 at most, in Avoid, however strong or light they are. When the label gives no figure, a drink sold as beer, wine, cider or spirits counts too. We score these drinks even when there’s no ingredient list. Then, if you’ve set allergens or a diet, the app tells you it can’t check the drink for them. Drinks with 0.5% or less, like alcohol-free beer, have no ceiling, and neither does food made with alcohol, like a rum cake or cooking wine. Both show on the Alcohol watch list, if you’ve picked it.

A product gets one ceiling at most, the strictest that applies. Your goals can lower a product under its ceiling, but never lift it above: organic plain chips with “Prefer organic” (+10) would reach 80, and score 75.

Four concern levels for ingredients

We rate additives, and other ingredients such as palm oil, when research points to a possible effect on health at the amounts found in food. Being processed isn’t enough on its own: that’s what the processing points are for. Each rated ingredient gets one of four levels.

Some concern−5—Natural flavors, maltodextrin, xanthan gum, phosphoric acid

Within High concern, the more serious the concern, the more points come off. And remember the processing points: most rated additives are level 3 or 4, which costs another 0.5 or 1.5 there. Natural flavors, for example, are Some concern and level 4, so they take off 6.5 points in total.

What a rating is about

A rating is about the evidence on that ingredient itself, from studies in people and in animals. It isn’t about how processed the ingredient is, because the processing points already count that, or about how much fat it brings, which your goals can look at. A few rules follow from that:

  • Oils are rated only for a specific hazard. Palm oil is High concern because of contaminants that form when it’s refined, and partially hydrogenated oils are High concern for their trans fats. Most seed oils, like sunflower, rapeseed (canola), soybean or corn oil, aren’t rated. Their processing level still counts.
  • An additive the label doesn’t name is rated one level stricter. Plain “thickener” could be one of many, so it’s Some concern, one level above common named ones like guar gum or pectin (Low concern). Plain “flavoring” is Some concern too, while a named one like vanillin is Low concern, and an extract that names its source, like garlic extract, isn’t rated.
  • One substance counts once. Guarana is rated for its caffeine, so a drink that lists both caffeine and guarana pays for its caffeine once, by the stricter rating. Both still show in the ingredient list, and each still costs its processing points. In the same way, nitrite counts once: when a meat lists both celery powder and sodium nitrite, the sodium nitrite is the one rated.
  • Celery powder that cures meat counts as nitrite. Meat sold as “uncured”, or with “no nitrates or nitrites added”, is often cured with celery powder or juice instead, and ends up with nitrite all the same. So in a cured meat, celery powder is rated High concern, like sodium nitrite. In a soup or a seasoning it’s just celery, and it isn’t rated. For now we only read this on English-language labels.

How a level is chosen

A level is our reading of the evidence: published studies, in people and in animals, and assessments by food-safety authorities such as EFSA in Europe, the FDA in the US and the WHO. New ratings are checked against the original documents and approved by our team before they reach the app, and we’re re-checking older ratings the same way. A rating can change when the evidence does, or when we take a fresh look at it.

A level isn’t a legal status. An ingredient can be allowed in food and still be rated Some or Moderate concern, because we look at the research, not just at what’s permitted.

In the app, tap any rated ingredient to read “Why this rating” in plain words. Tap “Show the research” for the longer story and, for nearly all ingredients, the sources we used.

Four score ranges

Every score lands in one of four ranges. The ranges describe the whole product; the concern levels above describe single ingredients.

RangeScoreUnder the score, the app says

Limit36–55Once a week or less
Avoid0–35Skip it when you can

That line gets personal when it needs to. If a product lists one of your allergens, including ones you type in, or we know it doesn’t fit your diet, it turns red and says “Not for you — see below” instead. If it may contain one of your allergens, it says “May contain your allergens — see below”. For a drink we score without an ingredient list, it asks you to check the label for your allergens or diet. A sugar, honey or syrup says “This is sugar: use it sparingly.” whatever its score, and chips in Occasional say “A now-and-then snack”. On a Low concern or Occasional product, it says “Check the label for your diet — see below” when we can’t confirm the product fits your diet, and otherwise names anything on your watch list. And a Low concern product with a Moderate concern ingredient says “Good overall — 1 ingredient worth a look”.

A high score means we found little to take points off. It doesn’t mean a product suits everyone. And an ingredient that isn’t in our database yet can’t take points off either, so the app tells you when it couldn’t recognize everything.

What’s personal, and what isn’t

Your food, your rules, but the base score is the same for everyone. Your settings work in two ways.

Your goals can move your score

Pick up to 5 of 9 goals. Together they can move your score by up to 15 points, up or down. They can lower a product with a cap or a ceiling, but they never lift it above that. On each product’s last page, the score breakdown shows “Score before your goals”, what each goal changed and why, and then “Your score”.

A “lower” goal does nothing until a product goes past the “low” level, then takes off more the further past it goes, up to 13 points. A “higher” goal does nothing until a product is a “source of” that nutrient, and adds up to 5 points when it’s “high in” it. The “low”, “source of” and “high in” levels come mostly from the EU’s rules for nutrition claims and the UK’s front-of-pack labels. Where there’s no official level, as for carbs or for where a “lower” goal has its full effect, we set our own.

Amounts are per 100 g, or per 100 ml for drinks. “Lower added sugar” uses the label’s added sugar when it gives one, and total sugar when it doesn’t. For mostly-fat foods like oils, nuts or cheese (17.5 g of fat or more per 100 g), “Lower saturated fat” looks at how much of the fat is saturated instead: nothing up to 20%, the full 13 points at 50%. “Lower calories” and “Higher calories” can’t be picked together.

Warnings never change the score

These show up as separate alerts by the score, and the number stays the same:

  • Allergens: gluten, dairy, eggs, tree nuts, peanuts, soy, fish, shellfish and sesame. For these, the app checks the ingredients, the label’s “Contains” line and Open Food Facts’ allergen data. You can also type in your own, and the app looks for them by name in the ingredient list and the label’s “Contains” line.
  • Gluten: we follow the EU’s list of cereals containing gluten, so wheat, rye, barley, triticale and oats are flagged, and so is what’s made from them, like malt, malt vinegar or beer. When oats are the only gluten we find and we recognize every ingredient, the app says “contains oats (gluten)”. EU law doesn’t require a gluten warning for glucose syrups made from wheat or barley, or for dextrose and maltodextrin made from wheat, because processing removes almost all of the gluten. On their own they get no warning, and “Good to know” says what they’re made from. Nor are spirit vinegar and alcohol (ethanol) flagged, because EU law also exempts cereals used to make distilled alcohol. Oats the label calls gluten-free, or oats in a product labeled gluten-free, get no warning either, but “Good to know” adds that some people with celiac disease also react to oats’ own protein, avenin, even in gluten-free oats. The warning stays, though, when the label’s own “Contains” line names gluten or a gluten cereal, like wheat or oats, and when Open Food Facts lists gluten but we can’t recognize every ingredient. Wheat starch is different: for now it keeps the warning even when it’s gluten-free, because it’s still wheat and people with a wheat allergy use the same Gluten option.
  • “May contain”: shown for your allergens, ones you type in too, in a softer box than a “contains”.
  • Diets: vegan, vegetarian, halal and kosher. When we know a product doesn’t fit, the app says “Not for you”. For halal that includes alcohol: an alcoholic drink, or alcohol the ingredient list names, like rum in a rum cake or alcohol listed in a vanilla extract. We go by what the list says, so a plain “vanilla extract” or “rum flavoring”, which can be made with alcohol, isn’t flagged, and neither is a vinegar. Some ingredients rarely say enough to tell, so for these the app shows a softer, yellow notice instead, such as “Can’t confirm it’s vegetarian”: cheese and rennet, which may come from an animal; and, for halal and kosher, gelatin and collagen whose animal isn’t named, and meat, since no ingredient list says how the animal was slaughtered. For halal and kosher it adds “Look for a certification.”, and a product that carries that diet’s certification gets no notice. Beyond these, no warning doesn’t mean a product fits.
  • Your watch list: 11 groups, such as artificial sweeteners, synthetic food dyes, seed oils or alcohol. Heavy metals are flagged for ingredients that can carry them; that isn’t a lab test of the product. Alcohol covers alcoholic drinks, their alcohol-free versions and food made with alcohol.

What we don’t score or rate, and why

  • Supplements aren’t scored. A supplement is taken in small doses for a specific purpose, so a food score wouldn’t be fair to it. You can still check its ingredients and allergen warnings. Protein powders, protein bars and meal replacements are eaten as food, so they are scored.
  • No ingredient list, no score, except for alcoholic drinks (see ceilings). If a product’s ingredients are missing, the app asks you to photograph them.
  • Plain foods aren’t rated. Milk, oats, flour, eggs and most everyday ingredients don’t get a concern level, and neither do most oils, protein isolates, yeast extracts, starches (nearly all modified starches too), most fibers or most plant extracts. A rating needs a reason in the research, and being processed isn’t one on its own. Their processing level still counts, though.
  • Added vitamins and minerals aren’t rated or counted. They don’t change the score at all. The app lists them together under “Good to know”.
  • Salt isn’t scored. It counts only if you pick the Lower sodium goal (see goals). We still tell you when there’s a lot: a product with more than 1.5 g of salt per 100 g, or a drink with more than 0.75 g per 100 ml, the UK’s front-of-pack levels for “high”, shows a grey line under the score, such as “High in salt: 2.8 g per 100 g”. It’s information only: it never changes the score or replaces any other line or alert.
  • Sugars aren’t rated. They’re counted by amount instead (see Added sugar), so what matters is how much is in a serving, not what the label calls it.
  • The strength of a drink isn’t scored. Any drink with more than 0.5% alcohol scores 35 at most, a light beer the same as a spirit. Under that ceiling, its ingredients count as they would in any other drink. When a food’s label lists alcohol as an ingredient, as vanilla extract often does, it’s rated Low concern, like any other rated ingredient.

Where the data comes from, and its limits

  • Open Food Facts. Most product details, including ingredients, nutrition, categories and the NOVA group, come from Open Food Facts, a free, collaborative database of food products, available under the Open Database License.
  • People who use Knowsie. When a product is missing or its label has changed, people photograph it. An AI model (Anthropic’s Claude) reads the photos, the person checks the details and submits them, and they get a score right away. Our team checks every submission before it’s added for everyone.
  • Our team. We correct products when their data is wrong, and we look after the ingredient database, with each ingredient’s other names, E-numbers and names in several languages.

To score a product, we match each ingredient on the label to our database. An ingredient we don’t recognize yet can’t change the score, so the app says so, for example “Score based on 5 of 6 recognized ingredients”, and lists the rest as “Not yet in our database”. We collect those names so we can add them.

Scores can change. When we update a rating, fix a product or add an ingredient, the score updates too. A product in your history is re-scored when you open it.

Please read the package. Labels can be incomplete, out of date or wrong, and recipes change. If you have a food allergy, always check the actual packaging. Knowsie is a helpful extra set of eyes, not a replacement for yours.

Not medical advice

Knowsie gives general information about food ingredients. It isn’t medical advice, it isn’t a medical device, and it doesn’t replace a doctor or dietitian. A score is our reading of the research, applied to the information on a label. It isn’t a guarantee about any product for any person. If you have an allergy or a health condition, follow your doctor’s advice.
